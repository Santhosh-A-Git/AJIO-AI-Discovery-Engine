2. Align the Engine to the Business Question
The central discovery question is: “Why do users who save/wishlist fashion products fail to convert that expressed interest into a purchase within 30 days?”
The engine must discover the underlying user problem. It must not assume that price, fit, reviews, delivery, or any other factor is the answer.

Do not perform deep structured analysis on obviously irrelevant records. Keep excluded records in the raw/audit dataset, marked as excluded.
4. Relevance Classifier
Add a dedicated classifier with three statuses:
•	RELEVANT: Directly connected to wishlist/save behavior, shortlisting, purchase postponement, purchase decision uncertainty, comparison, purchase/non-purchase after saving, external information seeking, or gifting/social/bookmarking behavior related to saved products.
•	POSSIBLY_RELEVANT: Useful online fashion-shopping decision behavior without a direct wishlist-to-purchase connection.
•	NOT_RELEVANT: Does not provide useful evidence for the target discovery question.
Store relevance_status, relevance_reason, and relevance_confidence. Never silently discard records without preserving the audit trail.
5. Distinguish Source Types
•	USER_GENERATED: Google Play, App Store, Reddit, YouTube comments, public fashion/shopping communities, public social posts, product reviews and Q&A.
•	BRAND_GENERATED: AJIO official replies, customer-support responses, AJIO marketing content.
•	SECONDARY_CONTEXT: news articles, reports, editorial articles.
User-generated evidence should be the primary evidence base for user-problem discovery. Do not mix brand-generated replies into user-prevalence calculations.

Allow emergent intent categories. Distinguish genuine purchase intent from bookmarking, exploration, comparison, gifting, sharing, and recommendation-driven saving.
8. Why Saved
Capture the explicit reason for saving. Examples include liked product, purchase later, waiting for sale, comparison, reminder, gifting, recommendation, sharing, inspiration, or avoiding loss of the item.
Do not infer a reason without evidence. If unsupported, use UNKNOWN.

Use this as an initial taxonomy only. Allow emergent categories. Do not make PRICE_VALUE the default or presumed winner.
10. Uncertainty
Capture what the user still does not know or does not feel confident about, such as sizing accuracy, fit for body type, product quality, authenticity, seller legitimacy, appearance, styling, price/value, occasion suitability, alternative comparison, or review trust.
If uncertainty is not identifiable from evidence, use UNKNOWN. Never manufacture uncertainty.
11. Workarounds and External Information Seeking
Persist workaround and external_platform_used. Capture sources such as Google, Instagram, YouTube, Reddit, Myntra, Amazon, Flipkart, friends/family, influencers, or other sources when explicitly mentioned.
Also capture what uncertainty or problem caused the user to seek that external information.
12. Purchase Status
PURCHASED
NOT_PURCHASED
DELAYED
ABANDONED
REMOVED
UNKNOWN
Do not infer purchase from positive sentiment or liking the item.
13. Evidence Strength
•	HIGH — Direct statement by the user.
•	MEDIUM — Strong contextual evidence but not explicitly stated.
•	LOW — AI inference or weakly supported interpretation.
Every evidence-strength label must be auditable. Where practical, explain why it is high/medium/low.
14. Preserve Original Evidence and Provenance
Every AI-generated insight must be traceable to the underlying source record. Preserve source platform, source URL, source ID, and original text or permitted excerpt.
Never use AI-generated paraphrases as if they were the original source citation. The UI should let a PM move from opportunity → theme → supporting observations → original evidence → source.
15. Deduplicate Evidence
At minimum, identical source + source_id records must count as one unique observation. Add semantic near-duplicate detection where practical.
Prevent duplicates from inflating volume, prevalence, opportunity scores, or source counts.
Expose clear data counts such as Raw Records, Unique Records, Relevant Observations, Possibly Relevant, Excluded, and Opportunity Clusters.
16. Replace Generic Purchase Intent with Wishlist Conversion Relevance
Do not rely primarily on generic keyword logic such as purchase, buy, checkout, order, refund, or money. Instead, create a wishlist_conversion_relevance signal based on the structured evidence and the journey: SAVE/WISHLIST → CONSIDERATION → DECISION → PURCHASE / DELAY / ABANDONMENT.
17. Opportunity Quantification and Comparison
Quantify where justified. Clearly define every denominator.
•	Evidence prevalence / count
•	Evidence share among relevant observations
•	Wishlist conversion relevance
•	Evidence strength
•	Severity where supported
•	Segment concentration
•	Cross-source consistency
•	Workaround prevalence
The opportunity score must be transparent, configurable, and explainable. Do not claim causal conversion impact unless causal evidence exists. Prefer wording such as “Potential relevance to wishlist conversion.”
18. Cross-Source Validation
For important themes, calculate the number of unique independent sources in which the theme appears and display that in the opportunity view. A theme recurring across multiple independent user-generated sources should receive stronger confidence than a theme found in only one source, all else equal.
19. Segment Analysis
Make user_segment_clue a first-class analytical field. Where sufficient evidence exists, show theme-by-segment, blocker-by-segment, intent-by-segment, and workaround-by-segment patterns. Never fabricate demographics.
20. Emergent Theme Discovery
Continue using semantic clustering. Do not constrain the engine to the initial taxonomy. Allow recurring evidence to create a new theme, but require supporting evidence, inspectability, and meaningful volume before treating it as a major opportunity.
21. Evidence-Safe Language
•	Replace “AI proved” with “The current analyzed dataset indicates…”
•	Replace overly causal headlines such as “Fit Ambiguity Halts Conversion” with “Fit Uncertainty Emerges as a Potential Conversion Barrier.”
•	Use “Evidence suggests…” when appropriate.
•	Do not claim that an observational public-feedback analysis has established causation.
22. Dashboard Changes
•	Keep the current visual design and interaction quality.
•	Rename “Live Feedback Stream” to “Evidence Explorer” or “User Feedback Evidence” unless it is genuinely real-time.
•	Keep the strong NEXTLEAP-aligned suggested PM queries.
•	Change the generic Ask AI placeholder to a wishlist-conversion research example.
•	Add evidence/relevance/source-type filters to the Feedback/Evidence view.
•	For each opportunity, display evidence volume, evidence share, wishlist relevance, source diversity, segment clues, common workarounds, purchase-status distribution, evidence strength, supporting evidence, and important unknowns.
23. Inventory Tab
The current product catalog contains hard-coded example product records. Do not present synthetic examples as real discoveries.
Preferred action: either make the tab genuinely data-driven from the evidence dataset or remove it from the final discovery experience. Do not prioritize this tab over evidence rigor.
24. Ask AI
Preserve the semantic-query capability, but constrain answers to retrieved evidence. Require supporting citations/observation references, explicit uncertainty when evidence is insufficient, and a strict distinction between fact and inference.
25. Report Generation
Make the generated report use the same canonical evidence dataset as the dashboard. It should report research scope, sources, counts, relevance filtering, intents, blockers, uncertainties, workarounds, cross-source themes, segment patterns, opportunity comparisons, evidence examples, and important unknowns.
Never present an AI hypothesis as a validated user problem.
26. Acceptance Test Cases
1.	Generic AJIO complaint: “AJIO customer support took three days to respond.” → NOT_RELEVANT unless a direct wishlist/purchase connection exists.
2.	Wishlist price delay: “I saved this dress but I am waiting for the price to drop.” → RELEVANT; deferred purchase; price/timing blocker; evidence strength HIGH; do not infer value dissatisfaction.
3.	Fit uncertainty: “I like the dress but do not know which size will fit me.” → RELEVANT; fit/size blocker; fit/size uncertainty; HIGH evidence.
4.	Bookmarking: “I save clothes I like so I can find them later.” → BOOKMARKING; do not label automatically as high purchase intent.
5.	Recommendation-driven saving: “My friend sent me this kurta, so I saved it.” → RECOMMENDATION_DRIVEN or SHARING; purchase status UNKNOWN unless stated.
6.	External validation: “Before I buy I check YouTube videos to see how the dress actually looks.” → external platform YouTube; workaround external validation; uncertainty only where supported.
7.	Brand reply: “We are sorry for the inconvenience. Our team will contact you.” → BRAND_GENERATED; exclude from user-prevalence calculations.
8.	Duplicate records with identical source ID → one unique observation.

30. Final Non-Negotiable Principle
DO NOT ASK: “What feature should we build?”
ASK: “What is preventing wishlist intent from becoming purchase?”

DO NOT START WITH: “Price is the problem.”
START WITH: “What patterns does the evidence reveal?”

DO NOT PRESENT: “AI proved the problem.”
PRESENT: “Evidence indicates a potential opportunity.”

DO NOT SHOW: AI-generated source citations.
SHOW: Original user evidence + provenance.

DO NOT OPTIMIZE FOR: the most common complaint.
OPTIMIZE FOR: the strongest evidence-backed opportunity relevant to the stated business outcome.
